### Blueprint Information

**Title**: Data and Analytics - Data-Driven Business Models: A Blueprint for Innovation
**Concept Name**: DDBM-Innovation Blueprint
**Authors**: Josh Brownlow, Mohamed Zaki, Andy Neely, Florian Urmetzer
**Publication Details**:
*   **Type**: Working Paper
*   **Date**: February 2015
*   **Publisher**: Cambridge Service Alliance
*   **Copyright**: © Cambridge Service Alliance 2015
**Summary Purpose**: The DDBM-Innovation Blueprint is an integrated framework designed to help organizations become data-driven by enabling them to construct their own Data-Driven Business Model (DDBM) in coordination with six fundamental questions for a data-driven business. It provides a research-based guide for existing businesses to create and implement their own DDBMs, aiding in identifying and mitigating inhibitors to effective DDBM construction and implementation.
**Methodology**:
*   **Development**: Academically secured, research-grounded, and industry-focused constructs. Concepts developed using publicly available company documents.
*   **Validation**: Achieved through interviews, surveys, and a workshop with data-oriented company representatives. Further validation obtained by analyzing current data-driven businesses and their DDBMs against the blueprint and corresponding questions.
*   **Research Approach**: To create an effective guide for businesses to implement DDBMs, the main constituents and operations of DDBMs in start-ups and established businesses were identified. Organizations were chosen randomly through literature reference frequency. Established businesses were selected from five sectors (finance, insurance, publishing, retail, telecoms), and start-up samples were taken from AngelList, focusing on 'big data' or 'big data analytics' categories.
**Six Fundamental Questions for DDBM**:
1.  What do we want to achieve by using big data? (Target Outcome)
2.  What is our desired offering? (Offering)
3.  What data do we require and how are we going to acquire it? (Data Source)
4.  In what ways are we going to process and apply this data? (Key Activities)
5.  How are we going to monetize it? (Revenue Model)
6.  What are the barriers to us accomplishing our goal? (Inhibitors)
**Managerial Implications**:
*   Existing businesses can follow a step-by-step process to construct their own DDBM, centered around their desired outcomes, organization dynamics, resources, skills, and business sector.
*   Businesses can identify common inhibitors to constructing and implementing an effective DDBM within their own organization and plan to mitigate them.
*   Further inspiration and guidance can be gained by studying examples of current data-driven businesses and their DDBMs, as outlined in Table 1, and by conducting their own research for aligned DDBM examples.
**Inhibitors**:
*   **Description**: Barriers to accomplishing DDBM goals, color-coded and ranked from severe (red) to minor (green) based on frequency and severity as perceived by 41 strategy and data-oriented elite interviewees.
*   **Examples from Table 1**:
    *   Cultural problems, value perception of a DDBM (e.g., Zara)
    *   Data privacy obstacles, cultural problems (e.g., AT&T)
    *   Cultural problems, value perception, personnel issues (e.g., The New York Times)
    *   Data availability and accessibility, departmental collaboration issues (e.g., ING Direct)
    *   Data quality and integrity, legal obstacles (e.g., Goldman Sachs)
    *   Data quality and integrity issues (e.g., Swarmly, Next Big Sound)
    *   Legal challenges (e.g., Gild)
    *   Data accessibility and integrity (e.g., Welovroi)
    *   Cultural problems, data privacy obstacles (e.g., FarmLogs)
**Data Sources**:
*   **Types Identified**: Free available data, Customer-provided data, Existing data, Acquired data, Self-generated data, External data, Internal data
*   **Utilization by Established Businesses**: Customer-provided and acquired data were utilized by 80% of analyzed businesses. Self-generated and existing data utilization was slightly lower at 75%. Free available data was the least exploited, used by 60%.
*   **Significance by Sector**:
    *   **Telecommunications**: Considers self-generated data most significant, with particular emphasis.
    *   **Retail**: Considers self-generated data most significant, with particular emphasis.
    *   **Financial Services**: Considers self-generated data most significant.
    *   **All Sectors**: Customer-provided data is utilized and regarded as important across all analyzed sectors.
**Offerings**:
*   **Types**: Raw data (set of facts without attached meaning), Information or knowledge (interpreted data, output of analytics), Non-data product or service (value proposition enhanced by data)
*   **Established Organizations Offerings**:
    *   **Non-data product or service**: 100% of analyzed established organizations had this offering.
    *   **Information and knowledge**: 24% of analyzed established organizations had this offering.
    *   **Data as offering**: 20% of analyzed established organizations had this offering.
    *   **Dominant by Sector**:
        *   **Retail/Insurance**: 100% non-data product or service.
        *   **Publishing**: 48% non-data product or service, over 35% data as an offering.
        *   **Finance**: 85% non-data product or service.
        *   **Telecommunications**: 50% data as an offering.
*   **Start-up Offerings**: Tend to create entirely new offerings. A noteworthy predominance of B2B business models. Majority offer information or knowledge, often web-based.
**Target Customers**:
*   **Segmentation**: Businesses (B2B), Individual consumers (B2C), Consumer to Consumer (C2C) - facilitating use of customers to acquire further customers
*   **Established Organizations Targeting**:
    *   **B2C**: Dominant target customer for 75% of analyzed companies.
    *   **B2B**: Targeted by 50% of established organizations.
    *   **C2C**: Utilized least, with 20% referencing this activity.
    *   **Dominant by Sector**:
        *   **Retail/Insurance**: B2C dominant (76% and 54% respectively).
        *   **Publishing**: B2B referenced by 61%. C2C <20%.
        *   **Finance**: B2B referenced by 72%.
        *   **Telecommunications**: C2C dominant (45% of references).

### BigQuery Best Practices

**1. Performance Optimization**
*   **Optimize Query Performance**
    *   Avoid SELECT \* and select only necessary columns to reduce the amount of data processed.
    *   Filter data as early as possible using WHERE clauses to reduce scanned data.
    *   Use clustering and partitioning for large tables to improve query performance and reduce cost.
    *   Avoid data anti-patterns like self-joins, cross-joins, and expensive regular expressions when possible.
    *   Utilize materialized views for frequently accessed and complex queries to pre-compute results.
    *   Cache query results by saving results to a new table or enabling cached results for repeated queries.
    *   Optimize join order, placing the largest table last and filtering it first.
    *   Use approximate aggregate functions (e.g., APPROX\_COUNT\_DISTINCT) when exact counts are not critical for faster results.
    *   Monitor query execution plans and query history to identify bottlenecks and optimize.
    *   Limit the number of concurrent queries if contention is observed.
*   **Schema Design**
    *   Use appropriate data types; for example, use DATE, TIMESTAMP, or INT64 instead of STRING when possible.
    *   Denormalize data where appropriate to avoid complex joins and improve query performance, especially for analytics.
    *   Consider nested and repeated fields (STRUCTs and ARRAYs) to represent hierarchical data and reduce table size.
    *   Avoid over-partitioning or under-partitioning tables. Choose partitioning columns wisely based on query patterns.
    *   Cluster tables on columns frequently used in WHERE clauses or JOIN conditions.
*   **Data Ingestion and Storage**
    *   Ingest data in larger batches rather than small, frequent insertions to optimize loading jobs.
    *   Use Avro, Parquet, or ORC formats for data loading as they are column-oriented and often more efficient than CSV or JSON.
    *   Consider using streaming inserts for near real-time data analysis, but be aware of associated costs and quotas.
    *   Utilize partitioning and clustering at ingestion time to organize data for better query performance and cost.

**2. Cost Optimization**
*   **Query Cost Management**
    *   Preview queries before running them to estimate the amount of data that will be processed.
    *   Avoid SELECT \* and specify only the columns needed to minimize scanned data, directly impacting cost.
    *   Partition and cluster tables to reduce the amount of data scanned by queries.
    *   Use destination tables for query results if the same result set is queried repeatedly.
    *   Utilize materialized views to pre-compute and store frequently accessed query results, reducing subsequent query costs.
    *   Set cost controls and custom quotas at the project or user level to prevent unexpected spending.
*   **Storage Cost Management**
    *   Choose appropriate storage tiers; consider using long-term storage for infrequently accessed data.
    *   Delete unnecessary tables or data.
    *   Design schemas efficiently, using optimal data types and nested fields to reduce storage footprint.
    *   Compress data before loading if possible, although BigQuery automatically handles compression.

**3. Security and Data Governance**
*   **Access Control**
    *   Implement principle of least privilege using Identity and Access Management (IAM) roles.
    *   Grant access at the dataset, table, or column level using authorized views and row-level security.
    *   Audit BigQuery access and changes using Cloud Audit Logs.
    *   Use Service Accounts for programmatic access and manage their permissions carefully.
*   **Data Protection**
    *   Encrypt data at rest and in transit. BigQuery encrypts data at rest by default.
    *   Utilize Customer-Managed Encryption Keys (CMEK) for additional control over encryption keys.
    *   Implement Data Loss Prevention (DLP) to scan and de-identify sensitive data.
    *   Understand and comply with data residency requirements by selecting appropriate dataset locations.

**4. Data Management and Organization**
*   **Dataset and Project Organization**
    *   Organize datasets logically within projects based on business units, data domains, or environments (e.g., dev, test, prod).
    *   Use clear and consistent naming conventions for projects, datasets, tables, and columns.
    *   Leverage project hierarchy and resource labels for better management and cost allocation.
*   **Data Lifecycle Management**
    *   Implement data retention policies to automatically delete old data.
    *   Back up critical data, although BigQuery provides built-in durability. Consider table snapshots for point-in-time recovery.
    *   Version control your schema changes using tools like data build tool (dbt) or similar.
